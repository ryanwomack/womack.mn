from Sargasso to EunGyo

2026-08-29

Some snapshots in time can reflect an intellectual passage, even if they are somewhat tenuously linked.

Back in the 1990s, I read the Geometric Regional Novel by Gert Jonke when it first came out, during one of the periods when I was actively seeking new literature. I remember enjoying it, but now I am suspicious of fiction that is used to either make intellectual arguments or, as is far more common than I used to think, flatter the reader into thinking that they are an intellectual.

The filters I apply have shifted towards the works that express an emotional core, as evident elsewhere on this blog.

Thanks to the aforementioned influential person, I made my way back to Wide Sargasso Sea, consuming it in an ideal setting, curled up in bed during my travels. Like Quartet, it is dense, emotional, and inspirational for emulation, one of a few books that I realized I would be rereading again as soon as I had finished it. Detaching from the Jane Eyre connection helped greatly in opening me to it. So much so that I now talk about it and have been getting others to read it, a useful form of distraction.

Wide Sargasso Sea was followed by Revolutionary Road, which I was able to complete expeditiously due to the elongation of my journey. This was another “catch-up” read that I somehow finally was impelled towards by my efforts to compress my shelf. It is a useful antidote to the nauseating Updike that I recently sampled, and, while not entirely enamored of the content, I was drawn along by the style, which is also worthy of further study. As in Wright and few other favorites, the clarity and unpretentiousness of the sentences allows the sophistication of the thought and expression to flow easily into the reader’s mind. So, I will be going further down the Yates path.

The same trip led to further consumption of Despentes, which will certainly continue.

In the French memoir category, I read a review that suggested that Pelicot’s memoir Et la joie de vivre had some depth, which led me to read it, hoping it might be as affecting as Grinberg’s Respect, which was closely connected to my disenchantment with Blier. Without a desire to publicly delve into the details, I was disappointed by Pelicot, yet that led to a recommendation for Neige Sinno’s Triste tigre, which initial sampling led to a prompt purchase, of greater promise.

Independently, I had a delightful time reading Kitten by Stacey Yu, which also led my discovery that the ‘weird girl’ genre of literature had been categorized. Of the titles mentioned by that “Woman of Letters”, I had enjoyed Convenience Store Woman (but not later Murata), and been put off by Mosfegh, having picked up several of her titles on the basis of exactly the kind of book reviews that I like, only to find that they were very artificially writerly. I have discovered rather late in life a distinction I should have made earlier, that I dislike writers who make up stories just because they find making up stories interesting, and instead am drawn to those who are working out issues of their own in fictional form. This is probably implicit in my approach to many things, but it only crystallized recently to the point that I wish for a checkbox online that would let me sort “real fiction” from “made-up fiction”, as absurd as that sounds.

Anyway, back to Kitten, which although using an obviously played up scenario, had some beautiful writing in it that was as real as real can be when put under the microscope, and threw me into the kind of entwined reflection that is one of the great pleasures of fiction. My writing won’t happen so easily, I’m afraid.

I don’t want this to read like a book review blog, but the titles mentioned have been influential for me, reflections from the surface illuminating the depths.

Finally, in a certain mood, I finally watched the film of Eungyo (은교), something I had known about for a decade having watched the Hyuna SNL sketch which rather accurately summarizes and skewers the film. The film suffers from one of the common defects of K-film: not that all Korean film is like that, but a high proportion has an admirable focus on realism and ordinary life, which makes directors blind to the inherent boredom and lack of imagination of having characters sit around looking out windows, walk down streets, and generally drag out the action in highly ‘realistic’ ways. Eungyo adds to this an uncomfortable plot and exploitative visual treatment of the teen lead that makes the viewer queasy, while still using that to probe complex ideas. As a representation of cringe in art, it was the right timing for me. The English version of the film title is apropos to the moment.

In the usual ‘way leads to way’ that overfills my lists, I discovered that I will have to see Exhuma, also with Kim Go-eun, and that although Eungyo the story has not been translated into English, the author Park Bum-shin (박범신) has a French Putain de pupitres (더러운책상), of all the titles! Anyway, that one has been requested for reading.
