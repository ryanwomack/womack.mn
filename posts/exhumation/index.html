exhumation

2026-09-25

Exhuma, a brilliant and exciting film with Kim Go-Eun of Cheese in the Trap fame and Choi Min-sik of Failan fame (at least to me, Failan is a real touchstone of what I am after), also delves deep into the ground, the spirit world, highlighting those powers and connections that can have strong effects across time and space. Exhumation allows us to dig up deeply buried things that trouble us in order to, as the film implies, bury them better the next time, which leads me to the subject of this post.

In life, even without considering the vast billions of unknown humanity, an individual has a large circle of known people. Off the top of my head (this is a blog after all, and I am not trying to verify these numbers), a person might bump up against ten to twenty thousand people in a lifetime, particularly if one is speaking to audiences or engaging in large group settings. Of those, maybe hundreds or a low number of thousands remain recognizable individuals whose name and characteristics might be partially remembered. I’m sure social media can more precisely quantify this. My own diary can confirm a few names written down whose faces are forgotten. Then there is the category that shades across colleague, acquaintance, friend (perhaps nemesis too), and other labels: a number in the lower hundreds of people who one might not mind spending time with, even pass pleasant days among.

But I maintain that this “ordinary circle” is still subject to indifference. For instance, while I might be happy to hear from anyone in this group, if I did not, or failed to receive a reponse to a communication over a period of months or years, the result would be no more than a couple of thoughts once in a while like “maybe they’ve moved”, or “maybe their e-mail has changed”, or “maybe they’re dead, comatose, or paralyzed”, or “maybe they’re in rehab, or “maybe they hate me”, or “maybe they’ve blocked me”, or “maybe their partner has prohibited communication”, and so on. These thoughts would flit into and out of the mind and pass on.

However, there is an inner circle, for whom one (at least this one) can never attain this Buddhist ideal of indifference. The thoughts cannot be swatted away. For this small group, whether the other person communicates or not becomes enormously consequential. In my case, that group could be counted on my fingers. I suppose if my cats were included, it would hit double digits, but my cats engage in no long-distance communication, and yet have never refused to communicate with me in person. So this dilemma does not involve the forever imperturbably impenetrable cats. But for the human members of the inner circle, the feelings generated by a gap in communication and the other’s unknown state is more akin to what Rumi repeatedly invokes as separation in the Masnavi,

I am weary of my life in separation. How long will the anguish of separation be killing me?

This is the issue that I have been trying to dig up, rectify, and rebury so that I can move on to a normal life, untroubled by the restless ghosts of the past.
